    Creative writing is writing meant to make someone feel something, not just know something. A report tells you what happened. A story puts you inside it.

    Same fact, two ways

    Forms of creative writing

    Creative writing comes in many shapes: short stories, poems, scripts for film and theater, creative nonfiction (true stories told with the craft of fiction), and audio like podcasts. In this course you’ll make a short story, a podcast, or a film.

    Five tools you’ll use all week

    Specific detail

    “A car” is nothing. “A rust-colored Buick with one hubcap” is a car in a story. The right detail tells the reader who owns it, how old it is, and what kind of place this is.

    The five senses

    Most beginners write what things look like. Add what it sounds like, smells like, and feels like on the skin. Horror lives in sound and smell more than you’d think.

    Voice

    Voice is how your narrator talks. A nervous night guard, a bored tour guide, and a kid telling it years later all describe the same hallway differently.

    Conflict

    Someone wants something, and something is in the way. No conflict, no story. You’ll plan this carefully in the Draft Lab.

    Show, don’t tell

    Don’t write “he was scared.” Write what scared looks like: the peeled label, the checked watch, the step back. Try the Show It game to practice.

    Horror is fiction built to make you afraid. It works by threatening something the reader cares about: safety, home, sanity, the people they love, or their sense of who they are.

    Terror and horror are different

    Terror is the dread before

    The creak on the stairs. The door that’s ajar. Your mind fills the gap with something worse than anything you could write. This is the good stuff.

    Horror is the shock after

    The thing finally shows up. It hits fast and fades fast. The novelist Ann Radcliffe argued that terror expands the mind while horror freezes it, and most writers still follow her lead.

    Five levers that make readers afraid

    The unknown

    What we can’t see is scarier than what we can. Describe the sound, not the source.

    The familiar made wrong

    A kitchen is safe. A kitchen where every cabinet is open and you didn’t open them is not. Start normal so the wrongness has something to break.

    A rule that breaks

    Readers trust the rules of a place: doors lock, lights work, the dead stay dead. Show a rule, then break it.

    A slow build

    One small wrong thing, then two, then a pattern. Escalate in steps. Readers feel dread when they see the pattern before the character does.

    Nobody believes it

    If someone says “you’re imagining it,” the character is alone with the problem. Isolation turns a strange event into a frightening one.

    Neighborhoods of horror

    Ghost stories, Gothic (old houses, family secrets), folk horror (old customs in rural places), found footage (told through recordings), psychological horror (is it real, or in their head?), and internet horror. You’ll try several this week.

    Class guideline: Scary comes from dread and imagination, not gore. Keep it school-appropriate.

    A ghost story is a story about unfinished business. Someone, or something, is stuck, and a living person has to figure out why.

    The five ingredients

    A place with a past

    The best haunted places have real history: a prison, a hotel, a hospital wing, a hall built on older ground. The place remembers.

    A ghost who wants something

    Don’t make it a random monster. What did this ghost want, lose, or never say? The answer is your ending.

    A rule

    Hauntings have patterns. It only happens after midnight. It only follows people who lie. Rules make it feel real and give you something to break.

    A witness

    The living character who notices. They’re skeptical, or they’re hiding something, and the ghost forces them to change.

    A last image

    End on something the reader can picture, not an explanation. One cold, specific image beats a paragraph of wrap-up.

    History, science, and legend

    History

    What actually happened here? Dates, names, records. This is where your story gets its weight.

    Science

    What could explain it? Drafty buildings, expectation, and low-frequency sound all make people feel watched. In one famous case, an engineer traced a lab’s “ghost” to low-frequency vibration from a fan.

    Legend

    What do people say? Legends are what the town tells itself. Use them, and decide whether your narrator believes them.

    Build it in the order readers feel it

    Normal, then one small wrong thing, then closer, then someone doesn’t believe it, then the rule is revealed, then the last image. That’s the shape the Twist Chain game uses. Try it, then open the Draft Lab to plan your own.

    A genre is a category of stories that share ingredients and promise the reader a certain feeling. Pick up a mystery and you expect a puzzle. Pick up a horror story and you expect to be unsettled. That promise is what makes genres useful.

    Genre, subgenre, and trope

    Genre

    The big category. Example: horror.

    Subgenre

    A smaller category inside it. Examples: ghost story, haunted house, Gothic.

    Trope

    A familiar device that shows up again and again. Examples: the cold spot, the creepy doll, the character who goes down to the basement alone.

    Why genres matter to writers

    Genres give you a map. Readers arrive with expectations, called conventions, and you can either deliver them or twist them. A ghost story where the ghost is kind is a twist. A mystery where the detective is also the culprit is a twist. You can’t twist a convention you don’t know.

    Stories can also belong to more than one genre. Horror plus history is a ghost story built on real events. Horror plus comedy is a spooky laugh. Our course is mostly horror plus history plus mystery.

    Genre is not the same as format or age group. Short story, podcast, and film are formats. Young adult and middle grade are age categories. A young adult book can be fantasy, mystery, or horror.

    Name that genre

    Here are ten of the most popular genres, each with a quick way to spot it and an idea for mixing it with horror. Open any one.

    Fantasy

    What it is: Magic, invented worlds, and quests. The rules of the real world don’t apply.

    You’ll know it by: Wizards, dragons, prophecies, maps at the front of the book.

    Mix it with horror: Dark fantasy. The magic has a price, and the monsters are old.

    Science fiction

    What it is: Stories built on imagined science and technology, often set in the future or in space.

    You’ll know it by: Spaceships, robots, time travel, “what if” questions about technology.

    Mix it with horror: A ship, a lab, or an AI that shouldn’t be awake.

    Mystery

    What it is: A puzzle at the center: a crime or a secret, with clues, suspects, and a solution.

    You’ll know it by: A detective, red herrings, a final reveal.

    Mix it with horror: Every haunting is a mystery: why is the ghost here, and what does it want?

    Thriller and suspense

    What it is: High stakes, fast pacing, and danger that keeps tightening.

    You’ll know it by: A ticking clock, a hunted hero, cliffhanger chapter endings.

    Mix it with horror: Survive the night, with something worse than a person chasing you.

    Romance

    What it is: A relationship is the heart of the story, and the ending is hopeful.

    You’ll know it by: Two people, obstacles between them, and a lot of feeling.

    Mix it with horror: Gothic romance, where a love story is haunted by a past.

    Historical fiction

    What it is: Invented characters living in a real time and place, often alongside real events.

    You’ll know it by: Period detail, real dates, and a setting that feels researched.

    Mix it with horror: A ghost story rooted in real local history. That’s Haunted Cincinnati.

    Horror

    What it is: Stories built to frighten, unsettle, or fill the reader with dread.

    You’ll know it by: Threat, atmosphere, and the sense that something is wrong.

    Our course: This is our home genre. See the Horror tab.

    Contemporary realistic fiction

    What it is: Everyday life, today, with no magic or monsters. The conflict comes from real situations and feelings.

    You’ll know it by: Familiar settings, such as school, family, and friends.

    Mix it with horror: Psychological horror. Real fears, with just a little wrongness at the edges.

    Adventure

    What it is: Journeys, risk, and exploration, where the action drives the story.

    You’ll know it by: Expeditions, survival, escapes, treasure.

    Mix it with horror: An expedition into a place that doesn’t want to be explored.

    Dystopian

    What it is: A grim imagined society where something has gone badly wrong, often under tight control.

    You’ll know it by: Surveillance, rules no one questions, a hidden truth.

    Mix it with horror: A perfect-looking society that hides a terrible secret.

    We’re a Catholic school, so it’s fair to ask: what does the Church actually say about ghosts and the paranormal? The honest answer is that some things are settled, some are open, and some are clearly off-limits.

    This is a student-friendly summary, not an official statement of the Church. If you have questions about faith, talk with your theology teacher, campus ministry, or a priest.

    What the Church teaches clearly

    • Every person’s soul is judged at death. The Church teaches that each soul goes to heaven, purgatory, or hell (Catechism 1021–1022, 1030).
    • The living and the dead stay connected. This is the “communion of saints.” Catholics pray for those who have died, and the Church sets aside All Souls’ Day, November 2, for exactly that (Catechism 1032).
    • Demons are real. The Church teaches that they are fallen angels who work against God (Catechism 391–395).

    What the Church leaves open

    There is no binding Church teaching on whether ghosts exist or what they are. Theologians have disagreed for centuries: St. Augustine was doubtful about most ghost stories, while St. Thomas Aquinas argued that souls are not locked away from the world. The Church has affirmed many apparitions of saints, but reports like these are “private revelation,” which means Catholics are not required to believe any particular story.

    A common Catholic view is that if a soul in purgatory ever appears, it would be by God’s permission and usually to ask for prayer, not to terrify anyone. Many Catholic writers also say that a violent or hateful “haunting” is more likely to be demonic than a human soul. These are traditions and opinions, not doctrine.

    Marian apparitions, like those at Lourdes and Fatima, are a different thing from ghosts. They are visits from heaven with a message, and they’re treated very differently from hauntings.

    What the Church warns against

    The Catechism says all forms of divination are to be rejected. That includes conjuring up the dead and consulting mediums, because it seeks power or knowledge outside of God (Catechism 2116). It also warns against magic and sorcery (2117). Catholic dioceses and writers have specifically cautioned about séances and Ouija boards for the same reason.

    What do Catholics do if they think they’ve met a ghost?

    Guidance from Catholic writers is fairly consistent:

    • Don’t try to communicate or bargain. Don’t treat it like a conversation.
    • Pray. If it might be a soul, offer prayers (and a Mass) for the dead. If it might be something worse, prayer is still the right response.
    • Talk to a priest if something keeps happening or feels frightening. Priests can bless homes.
    • Exorcism is rare and serious. Church law requires the bishop’s permission, and the Church’s approach is to rule out medical and psychological explanations first.

    Why this matters for investigators

    The Church has a long tradition of testing strange claims carefully, and it doesn’t assume the supernatural first. That’s the same mindset as your Spring Grove hunt: gather evidence, look for natural explanations, and only then ask what’s left over.

    Halloween has Catholic roots

    The word “Halloween” comes from All Hallows’ Eve, the evening before All Saints’ Day (November 1). The next day, All Souls’ Day, is for praying for the faithful departed. Some medieval Catholics believed souls in purgatory could appear around these feasts, which is one reason ghost stories and the Church’s calendar have always overlapped.

    Catholic stories about the dead

    Dante’s Divine Comedy is a famous journey through hell, purgatory, and heaven that is full of conversations with the dead. Much of the Western ghost-story tradition grew up around Catholic ideas of unfinished business, guilt, and prayer. That’s rich material for a writer.

    How this fits our course

    In Haunted Ohio, ghosts are stories, folklore, and history. Our fieldwork is observation and testing, and none of our activities involve trying to contact spirits, using Ouija boards, or holding séances.

      How to play

      Twist Chain

      How to play
      1. Press Start the chain to get an opening line and five rounds.

      What the pros say about writing horror

      Summarized from Reedsy’s guides. Read the originals below.

      Fear comes first

      People read horror to be unsettled, so the fear has to land. Everything else (setting, plot, voice) is there to build it.

      The uncertainty is the scare

      We know vampires and ghosts aren’t real, but the “what if they are?” is what shakes us. The uncanny, something familiar that feels wrong, works the same way. If you write the supernatural, make it convincing.

      Let your story carry a real worry

      Many great horror stories turn a real tension in society into a monster or a rule. Shirley Jackson’s short story “The Lottery” is a famous example. Ask what your haunting is really about.

      Give the threat a human heart

      The stories that keep people up at night are usually about something human, like grief, family secrets, or isolation, with something terrible growing inside it.

      Make the flaw feed the fear

      The thing that makes your character sympathetic should also be what makes them vulnerable. A person in denial is easy prey for a horror that thrives on disbelief. That’s your misbelief from the Draft Lab at work.

        Spring Grove field investigation

        Spring Grove Cemetery and Arboretum in Cincinnati opened in 1845, and it has more than its share of ghost stories. Your team will test four of them like a paranormal investigator and a skeptic at the same time, then bring the best material home for your own horror stories.

        Field rules. Stay on the paths. Respect all memorials. Follow teacher and staff instructions. Carry your phone and clipboard. Do not touch memorials unless you’re explicitly allowed to. Never shine a light at people.

        Every haunted place has two stories: what people say, and what the record says. The best horror writers use both. Read the four case files before you go.

        The watching eyes

        Lot 100, C.C. Breuer’s monument

        The legend
        A bronze bust on Breuer’s monument has eyes that seem to follow you. Some retellings say they’re his real eyes, preserved in glass at his request.
        The record
        The eyes are glass. Cincinnati Magazine reports that Breuer was a salesman and real estate investor, not the optometrist many retellings claim, so part of the legend has been wrong for a long time.
        A writer’s hook
        Why would a story about eyes keep getting retold even after the facts change?

        The white dogs

        Dexter Mausoleum, Section 20

        The legend
        Sit on the steps and two white dogs (some tellers say wolves) run past. Sometimes they stop and stare, glowing.
        The record
        The mausoleum is a Gothic Revival building from the late 1860s, built for the family of Edmund Dexter, a Cincinnati liquor distributor. Construction stopped in 1869 and it was never finished. Tellers can’t agree on the animals, their number, or the color of their eyes.
        A writer’s hook
        A mansion for the dead that was never finished. What is still waiting to be completed?

        The ankle grabber

        Section 53

        The legend
        A groundskeeper working alone says something grabbed his pant leg.
        The record
        This one rests on a single report passed along in local ghost lore. No one has documented it. Your job is to find out what could actually catch an ankle in that spot.
        A writer’s hook
        What does it feel like when you’re sure something touched you and nothing is there?

        Voices and figures

        Section 16

        The legend
        Visitors report hearing voices and seeing figures in this part of the cemetery.
        The record
        Section 16 is also the resting place of William Kirk, whose death in 1884 sparked the Cincinnati Courthouse Riots. That gives the area real history for a writer to work with.
        A writer’s hook
        A place where one event changed a whole city. Who might still be listening?

        One more fact worth knowing: the cemetery’s own staff say Spring Grove isn’t haunted, and most reported sightings happen during regular daytime hours. That’s why this hunt takes place in daylight, and why your Daylight Pattern Log matters. Strange things are easy to believe in the dark. Can you find one that holds up in the sun?
